S.B. 20: The 2022 Telecom Tax Change Analysts Estimated Could Shift $19 Million a Year

The Legislature's own fiscal analysts said telecommunications companies could pay less, and everyone else could pay more. Here is what the records show, and what they don't.

The short version

The bill. In 2022 the Legislature passed S.B. 20, "Property Tax Amendments." Sen. Curtis Bramble was the primary sponsor. Rep. Adam Robertson was the House floor sponsor.
What changed. Starting Jan. 1, 2023, the State Tax Commission stopped valuing telecommunications companies' property. County assessors value their land and buildings. Their equipment was valued first by the Multicounty Appraisal Trust, and since 2026 by the STATS program manager. The values go to each county.
The estimate. The fiscal note said the change "could result in a tax shift": telecom companies could pay less and "all other filers" could pay more, about $19 million a year statewide.
The votes. Every recorded vote was unanimous among the members voting.
What we haven't found. Any public report since 2023 that measures whether the $19 million shift happened, or how big it was in Weber County.

Where it is

S.B. 20 applies statewide. Weber County is where we looked hardest. In 2022, the last year the state valued it, telecom property in Weber County was valued at $107,667,524 and charged $1,219,936 in property taxes.

Map: OpenStreetMap. Points are approximate.

How it works: who values telecom property

Utah has two ways of valuing property for taxes. Your county assessor values homes, land and most businesses ("locally assessed"). The State Tax Commission values big companies that cross county lines, like railroads, airlines, power companies and mines ("centrally assessed"). It values the whole company once, then splits the value among the counties.

The bill uses the state sales tax law's definition of a "telecommunications service provider" (Utah Code 59-12-102). It covers services such as phone, wireless and internet-based voice calls. It leaves out some things people may think of as telecom, such as cable TV programming.

An older debate

In 1997 the Legislature passed S.J.R. 10, "Resolution Urging Reevaluation of Centrally Assessed Telecommunications Providers." Sen. Howard Stephenson was the sponsor. It said Utah's tax policies "discriminate between locally assessed telecommunications providers and centrally assessed telecommunications providers," and that centrally assessed providers "annually appeal" the Tax Commission's values and "have successfully sued the state for excessive valuations."

As passed, it asked the Tax Commission to study ending "the current valuation method" for centrally assessed telecom property "in favor of alternative methods," and to report back before Dec. 1, 1997. (The introduced version said "higher valuation method"; the House changed it to "current.") It passed the Senate 24-2-3 and the House 58-5-12, and the Senate agreed to the House change 29-0-0. We have not found whether the Tax Commission sent the 1997 report.

Where the bill came from, and the votes

The bill began as a recommendation from the Revenue and Taxation Interim Committee, which voted 14 for, 0 against, 5 absent (committee note on the bill). It was filed Dec. 14, 2021, as "Telecommunications Tax Amendments." Over three rewrites it grew and was renamed "Property Tax Amendments." The final version also changed the primary residence exemption form and appeal rules, the energy supplier "fee in lieu," the "centrally assessed benchmark value" for new growth, and raised the multicounty assessing and collecting levy from .000012 to .000015.

What the fiscal note said

Every version of the bill carried a fiscal note from the Office of the Legislative Fiscal Analyst. The final version's note said:

The shiftThe change "could result in a tax shift." Telecom companies "could have reduced property tax burdens and all other filers could have increased property tax burdens." The total was "estimated to be a shift of approximately $19 million annually."
Local revenueIt "could reduce property tax revenue to local governments indefinitely," because total centrally assessed value could fall below a benchmark used to figure new-growth revenue.
Cost to countiesAbout $40,000 per appraisal and $1,000 per appeal. "The aggregate cost is unknown."
Cost to the Multicounty Appraisal TrustAbout $1 million a year to value telecom equipment.
The levy increaseAbout $965,100 a year statewide; about $0.58 a year on a $350,000 home and $3 a year on a $1 million business.
Energy feeThe fee-in-lieu change could mean "a tax increase to affected taxpayers of up to $5.8 million statewide."
State cost$84,200 a year, plus $25,000 one time, for the Tax Commission to audit telecom providers.

A fiscal note is an estimate made before a bill passes. It uses the word "could." It is not a measurement of what happened later.

What happened after 2023

The state's numbers. The Tax Commission's 2022 annual report lists centrally assessed telecommunications property at $2,254,445,535 statewide. In Weber County it was $107,667,524, about 4.8% of the state's telecom value. The 2023 report no longer has a telecom line. A footnote says, "As of 2023, Telecommunications is now assessed by the Counties."

In 2022, the report's Table 2D ("Property Taxes Charged Against Each Class of Utilities Property") shows $1,219,936 in taxes charged on telecom property in Weber County and $23,098,608 statewide. Those are taxes charged, based on county year-end reports. They are not a measure of the fiscal note's estimated shift.

Weber County's total centrally assessed value went from $911,311,486 in 2022 to $757,554,707 in 2023. Not all of that drop was telecom; other lines changed too, such as electric utilities and railroads. The state reports do not show what Weber's telecom property was valued at under the new system, so we can't compare before and after from these reports.

The Utah Association of Counties' page for the STATS program reports that in 2025 telecom filings grew from 70 to 79 accounts, "covering $3.2 billion in taxable assets." That figure is statewide and counted a different way than the state's old numbers, so it is not a straight before-and-after comparison.

The Legislative Auditor. In October 2025 the Office of the Legislative Auditor General published Report No. 2025-21, "A Performance Audit of Local Authorities' Property Tax and the Shifting Tax Burden." It found the tax burden on homeowners grew in every county it sampled. One reason it listed for changing centrally assessed values: "telecommunications properties are no longer being centrally assessed." It said in "some counties" that change "has been substantial enough to shift the tax burden to homeowners." It did not name those counties, give a dollar amount for the telecom part, or measure whether the $19 million estimate came true.

Later bills

2024 · S.B. 30Primary sponsor Sen. Daniel McCay. Would have changed how telecom property is valued. It did not pass.
2024 · H.B. 53, Property Valuation AmendmentsPrimary sponsor Rep. Norman Thurston; Senate floor sponsor Sen. Daniel McCay. Signed March 14, 2024 (Laws of Utah 2024, Chapter 315). Moved the yearly equipment filing deadline from May 15 to March 31, added a March 31 to May 31 window to fix filing mistakes, required values by county and tax area and tax notices by May 31, set payment for June 30, and changed appeal deadlines. These are paperwork and timing changes. They do not undo the 2022 change.
2026 · S.B. 206Primary sponsor Sen. Wayne Harper; House sponsor Rep. R. Neil Walter. Signed March 23, 2026. Moved reviewing and valuing telecom equipment filings from the Multicounty Appraisal Trust to the STATS program manager; those sections took effect May 6, 2026. Telecom equipment values still go to the counties.

Graded by the Collective Rubric

TransparencyThe bill went through an interim committee, two standing committees and floor votes in both houses. A fiscal note was published with each version, and each one said plainly that other taxpayers could pay more. The hearing recording is public.
Financial accountabilityThe fiscal note gave an estimate. We have not located a public analysis measuring the realized telecom-specific tax shift statewide and by county. The 2025 audit says the change shifted the burden to homeowners in some counties but does not put a number on it.
Environmental impactNone identified.
