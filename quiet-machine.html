01

What's happening

Over the past year, one Weber County entity after another has asked residents for more property tax: schools, cities, fire districts, sewer, and now water. Some of these increases were denied. Most were sizable.

02

What's happening at the same time

Separately, the county and state have set up several programs that take a share of the new tax money created by growth — new warehouses, new subdivisions — and commit it to specific development projects for years, before it ever reaches schools, fire, or water.

03

Why that matters

Growth is supposed to help pay for the roads, water, sewer, and emergency response it requires. When a large share of growth's new tax revenue is set aside elsewhere, existing taxpayers are left covering more of the gap.

04

Why you rarely hear about it

Tax increases require a public hearing you get notified about. Committing growth revenue to a project area usually doesn't — it's approved through board votes and agreements that don't come with the same notice.

The simple version

Think of it like your neighborhood's water bill. New neighbors move in and start paying for water too — that new money is supposed to help cover the shared system everyone uses. But under some of these deals, most of that new revenue is set aside for years to help pay for the new development itself, not the shared system. Meanwhile, your own water bill goes up because "the system needs more money."

From 2007 through 2021, the Weber Basin Water Conservancy District's property-tax rate stayed essentially flat. The district relied on water rates, bonding, and other revenue instead.

Then, starting in 2022, the pattern changed. Weber Basin pursued its first property-tax increase since 2007. In 2023 it sought 29.9%, citing infrastructure upgrades. Now, in 2026, it's proposing another 6.95% increase — about $1,738,797 more a year, pushing its estimated ad valorem revenue to roughly $26.75 million. The rate would rise from 0.000187 to 0.000200.

The public hearing is August 24, 2026, 6:30–7:30 p.m., WERC Building, 2837 E. Highway 193, Layton, per the district's public notice and Property Tax Impact Schedule. Weber Basin says the money would support conservation rebates and repayment on federal water infrastructure dating to the 1950s. The increase would apply across Box Elder, Davis, Weber, Morgan, and Summit counties.

Weber Basin isn't alone. During 2025, numerous Weber County taxing entities held Truth-in-Taxation hearings for additional revenue, per the combined Weber County notice:

Weber Fire District followed with a separate 23.6% proposal later in the year. Central Weber Sewer then scheduled a June 2026 hearing for additional fee increases — 8% for general service fees, 39% for septic hauling — per its hearing notice.

Each entity offered its own explanation: aging pipes, growing demand, equipment, staffing, federal funding changes, infrastructure at end of life. Taken separately, each request can be defended. Placed together on one timeline, they raise a bigger question: what happens when growth's tax revenue is committed to development before it reaches the entities expected to serve that growth?

Utah's Truth-in-Taxation system rests on a basic rule: when existing property values rise, a taxing entity's certified rate generally adjusts so it doesn't get an automatic windfall from inflation. Qualifying new growth is treated differently — it can expand the tax base without the same public process. If an entity wants more than the certified amount, it must advertise and hold a hearing. That hearing is the visible part of the system, described in the Legislature's overview of the property-tax system.

Tax-increment financing runs through a different channel. When a project area is created, the taxable value inside its boundary gets a baseline. As value rises, the new revenue above that baseline — the increment, or under Utah Inland Port Authority terms, the "property tax differential" — is redirected for project-area purposes instead of flowing to participating taxing entities in the ordinary way.

"Because the governments are redirecting future growth rather than increasing their current rates, the decision does not generate the same Truth-in-Taxation process residents see when a school, city, fire, sewer or water district asks for more money."

Supporters argue the development — and therefore the increment — might not exist without the public financing. That may be true in some cases. But the development still creates public-service demands: roads, water, sewer capacity, emergency response, schools, administration — while much of its new tax growth stays committed to the project area.

Weber County now contains at least seven significant development-finance programs or layers. Because the Promontory Commerce Center structure contains three separate public infrastructure districts, the list represents at least nine governmental entities once those PIDs are counted individually.

These were approved through different meetings, boards, statutes, and agreements. Residents were rarely shown their combined effect in one place. One board authorizes a project area. Another approves an interlocal agreement. A PID gets taxing and bonding authority. Years later, a water, sewer, school, or fire district holds a separate hearing to explain why it needs more. Each decision can be defended in isolation — the public is left to assemble the full ledger.

The Military Installation Development Authority was created in 2007 to promote development connected to military installations. Its first major project, Falcon Hill, sits on and around Hill Air Force Base. Per MIDA's own Falcon Hill Project Area page, its enhanced-use lease covers ~525 acres across Davis and Weber counties — touching Clearfield, Riverdale, Roy, and Sunset — and includes the Roy Innovation Center and Northrop Grumman's facility supporting the Sentinel ICBM program.

That puts part of Roy directly inside MIDA's project footprint. In 2025, Roy sought a 27.96% property-tax increase — about $1.42 million more a year, an estimated $108.10 more annually on a $432,000 home, per the Roy hearing notice. That proposal was later denied by the State Tax Commission.

The record doesn't establish how much Roy-generated tax increment MIDA received in a given year, or what services Roy provided to those properties. The overlap doesn't prove causation. But MIDA is not just a distant statewide example — it operates inside one of the cities on the county's tax-increase timeline. MIDA's reach has also grown well beyond Falcon Hill: its Military Recreation Facility Project Area near Jordanelle expanded from ~40 acres in 2012 to more than 5,000 acres today. In 2025, S.B. 333 created a parallel Major Sporting Event Venue Zone framework, letting approved zones redirect up to 75% of property-tax increment — a sign the underlying model keeps expanding into new policy areas.

The Utah Inland Port Authority runs a parallel differential structure across project areas statewide, including West Weber, Central Utah Agri-Park, Castle Country, and Ancient Sky. Per UIPA's own explanation: the existing baseline stays with local taxing entities; 75% of new growth is redirected to UIPA; 25% continues to those entities.

West Weber's project area covers roughly 9,000 acres, supported by three Promontory Commerce Center PIDs, sewer financing, and public-road investment, per the West Weber Project Area Plan. The three PCC PIDs may impose up to 0.005 per dollar of taxable value with a combined $150 million debt limit, per Weber County's creation notice.

County financial records also show a recurring payment of about $120,000 per month to a Gardner-affiliated entity holding undeveloped land inside the project area. The full contractual basis for that payment hasn't surfaced in the UIPA board records located so far — that doesn't establish impropriety, but it does establish that a substantial recurring expenditure still lacks a clearly located board record explaining exactly what's being purchased.

In January 2023, the Utah Water Quality Board authorized $5 million in federal ARPA funding for wastewater improvements in western Weber County. On June 25, 2024, the County Commission approved an ARPA subrecipient agreement with the Promontory Commerce Center PID — remaining Little Mountain lagoon-rehab money would pass through the county to the PID's lift station, per the county's Commission meeting archive.

So the sewer infrastructure didn't wait for West Weber's differential to start producing revenue — federal money, state administration, county pass-through, and a developer-associated PID were already building infrastructure for the industrial project. Meanwhile, West Weber's anticipated tax trigger slid from 2025 to 2026 to 2027. As of the records reviewed here, no West Weber parcel had begun generating differential revenue for UIPA — it remains a future claim on growth, backed by public infrastructure spending already underway.

Under the CRA agreements, Weber County committed 75% of its increment for 15 years; Weber School District, Weber Fire District, and Weber Basin each committed 50% for 15 years, per CRA meeting minutes. Weber Basin's projected contribution is roughly $965,000 over the agreement's life.

The separate Nordic Village PID then added its own taxing-and-bonding layer: $39.38 million in bonds issued in 2025, carrying a 6.5% rate and maturing in 2055, per the district's financial statements and 2026 budget. The CRA redirects participating-government revenue; the PID can impose its own taxes and repay bonds. Legally distinct — financially stacked on the same development.

Chromalox's CRA documents projected roughly $4.13 million in property-tax revenue for infrastructure, incentives, and administration, per the county's hearing notice. The final interlocal structure runs county participation 15 years, school and fire district participation 10 years, per the interlocal notice.

West Bench, an older layer anchored by the America First Credit Union campus, collected its increment from 2020 through 2025. These programs differ in size and duration; their common feature is that future development revenue gets assigned through project-specific agreements before the full cost of long-term public services is known.

Weber Basin's taxing boundary covers most of Weber County and overlaps several of these structures — it's a confirmed 50%-for-15-years participant in the Nordic Village CRA, and it appears in the history of West Bench. Its board includes Weber County Commissioner Gage Froerer, who also serves on the Central Weber Sewer board and the MIDA board, and is identified on Little Mountain Service Area's public agendas as a board member there too.

Froerer's boards touch different pieces of the same growth-and-infrastructure system: Weber Basin supplies water, Central Weber Sewer handles wastewater, MIDA governs project areas with dedicated public revenue, Little Mountain Service Area oversees water/sewer in western Weber County, and Weber County itself creates CRAs, authorizes PIDs, and negotiates infrastructure agreements.

Froerer's January 2025 conflict-of-interest disclosure listed Weber Basin, Central Weber Sewer, and Weber County — not MIDA, despite his documented service there. His January 2026 disclosure again listed Weber Basin and Central Weber Sewer, again omitting MIDA and Little Mountain Service Area. Utah Code §20A-11-1604 requires disclosure of paid or unpaid board positions, so the absence of pay wouldn't excuse the omission on its own. This doesn't prove intentional concealment — it does show the public forms didn't present the complete network of boards one commissioner sat on.

Not every project here is collecting tax increment today. West Weber hasn't triggered its differential — its revenue is still contingent on development and formal UIPA action. Nordic Village and West Bench are different: West Bench's collection period ran 2020–2025, and Nordic Village's agreements are approved and its PID has already issued $39.38 million in bonds.

That distinction rules out a simplistic claim that every current rate increase was caused by money already diverted from every development area. It doesn't eliminate the underlying policy question: some growth revenue is already collected or committed; other revenue is pledged before development and service demand fully materialize — while entities responsible for water, sewer, fire, and schools keep asking existing taxpayers and ratepayers for more.

Each entity's stated need may well be legitimate. Aging water systems still require replacement; fire districts still need people and equipment; sewer systems still need maintenance. Nor can this analysis assume every dollar of projected growth would exist without the incentives that produced it.

Not established by this record
  • That Weber Basin's 6.95% increase was caused by Nordic Village
  • That Central Weber Sewer's rates rose because of Promontory Commerce Center
  • That Falcon Hill caused Roy's attempted increase
  • That any single project area forced a Truth-in-Taxation hearing
Established by this record
  • Weber County and the state have created multiple layers that reserve future growth revenue for specific projects
  • Numerous entities responsible for ordinary services sought substantial increases in the same period
  • The public sees these two facts through entirely separate processes, one hearing at a time
"The central question is not whether growth creates new taxable value. It does. The question is who receives that value first, how long it remains committed, and who pays for the public services required in the meantime."
