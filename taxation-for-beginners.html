Taxation for Beginners

There isn't just one tax — there are several, and they work in completely different ways. Here's each one, explained simply.

Several different taxes, several different rules

When people say "taxes," they usually mean one of a handful of totally separate things. Mixing them up is the number one reason tax stories feel confusing. Here are the three you'll run into daily — the rest, including income tax and gas tax, are covered further down.

Property Tax
Tap to reveal
A yearly bill based on what your home or business is worth. It pays for schools, cities, counties, fire districts, and water districts. This is the tax most of our stories are about.
Sales Tax
Tap to reveal
A small percentage added every time you buy something — clothes, electronics, a haircut. You pay it at checkout, not once a year.
Food Tax
Tap to reveal
A special, lower version of sales tax that applies specifically to groceries. Utah is one of only a handful of states that still taxes groceries at all.

Property Tax: Who Gets a Slice

Here's something most people don't realize: your one property tax bill isn't going to just one place. It's actually several different governments, each charging their own rate — and your real notice shows every one of them as its own separate line, not blended together.

Picture a pizza

Imagine your property tax bill is a whole pizza. Your school district gets the biggest slice — usually well over half. Then the county takes a slice, your city or town takes a slice, and smaller slices go to things like the fire district, the water district, and the library. None of these governments sets the whole pizza — they each just claim their own slice of it, and your bill is all the slices added up.

What that actually looks like on your real notice

This is a simplified example, not an actual bill — but it shows the real structure: every taxing entity gets its own line, and only the very bottom row is the number you actually pay.

Sample Property Tax Notice
Weber County · Example only
Weber School District $1,842.10
Weber County $487.55
City / Town $312.20
Fire District $198.40
Weber Basin Water $61.75
Mosquito Abatement / Other Special Districts $28.90
Total Property Tax Due $2,930.90
Each row is a separate government charging its own rate. If any entity is proposing a Truth-in-Taxation increase, your real notice shows a second "proposed" column next to that entity's row specifically — not the whole bill.
What you will never see on this notice

Income tax, sales tax, and food tax don't appear anywhere on a property tax notice — they're collected in completely different ways (out of your paycheck, or at checkout) and go through entirely separate processes at entirely different times of year. This notice is property tax only.

That's also why one street can have neighbors with different tax bills for similarly priced homes — they might sit in different school district boundaries, different fire district boundaries, or different special-service-district boundaries, even if they share a city and county.

Same notice, but now with a PID

If your home sits inside a Public Infrastructure District, one more line gets added — and it can be substantial. This is the mechanism at the center of our Quiet Machine investigation.

Sample Property Tax Notice — Inside a PID
Weber County · Example only
Weber School District $1,842.10
Weber County $487.55
City / Town $312.20
Fire District $198.40
Weber Basin Water $61.75
Public Infrastructure District (PID) $1,150.00
Total Property Tax Due $4,052.00
The PID line pays off bonds the district issued to build infrastructure for that specific development — it's its own small government, with its own taxing and bonding authority, separate from the county, city, or school district above it. It's added on top of everything else, not instead of it, and it can run for decades until the bonds are paid off.
This line can move — up or down

Most PID assessments in Utah are tied to your property's own value, the same way regular property tax is — as the county reassesses your home or the development around you goes up or down in value, the PID line on your bill moves with it. It's not usually a flat fee locked in at one number for the life of the bonds. That also means a hot housing market can make a PID line jump noticeably from one year to the next, even if the underlying bonds and project haven't changed at all.

What about the "transportation fee" some cities charge?

Nearly a dozen Utah cities charge a Transportation Utility Fee — but it doesn't show up on your property tax notice at all. It's billed on your city utility bill instead, alongside water, sewer, and garbage — a separate charge, on a separate bill, going through a separate process. Utah's courts have specifically ruled it counts as a "fee" rather than a "tax" because of that separation. It even applies to properties that don't pay property tax at all, like churches and some nonprofits, since it's tied to using the roads, not to owning taxable property.

Commercial vs. Residential: Not Taxed the Same

Utah treats a home you live in very differently from a business property, even if they're worth the exact same amount.

🏠

Your primary home

If it's the home you actually live in, Utah only taxes 55% of its value. The other 45% is exempt automatically. So a $500,000 primary home is really only taxed as if it were worth $275,000.

🏢

A business or commercial building

There's no exemption. A $500,000 commercial building is taxed on the full $500,000. Same goes for a second home, a vacation property, or a rental you don't live in yourself.

Why this matters

This is a huge, deliberate policy choice — homeowners pay tax on about half of what their house is worth, while a business next door pays on the whole thing. It's one reason property tax fights often involve arguments about whether new development is "commercial enough" to pull its weight, or whether it's quietly getting residential-style treatment.

There's a second gap too — how fast a reassessment happens

Beyond the exemption, there's a separate rule about how quickly a jump in value actually gets caught and taxed. A 2026 law (H.B. 77) set two different tripwires:

🏠

A home

Gets automatically flagged for reassessment once its value jumps 150% — so a big, sudden increase in what your house is worth gets caught and taxed relatively quickly.

🏢

A commercial property

Doesn't get that same automatic flag until its value jumps 350% — more than double the bar a home has to clear before the same review kicks in.

The original version of this bill set both thresholds at 150%; an amendment during the legislative process widened the commercial threshold specifically. It's not a dollar-for-dollar exemption like the 45% rule above — it's about how quickly a big value jump gets caught by the tax system at all.

What that actually means for a tax bill

Until a property crosses its trigger, the assessor isn't required to specially review and update its taxable value to match its real, current market value — so a property that's quietly worth a lot more than its assessed value keeps getting taxed on that lower, stale number. A home crossing 150% gets caught and moved to its real value relatively fast. A commercial property has to more than double that growth — all the way to 350% — before the same catch-up happens. In the gap between those two thresholds, a commercial property can sit on a significantly out-of-date, lower taxable value, and pay less tax than its real worth would otherwise require, for longer than a home in the same position ever could.

Food Tax: A Completely Different System

Food tax has nothing to do with property tax — it's a form of sales tax, charged every time you check out at the grocery store, not once a year on a bill.

🛒

Unprepared groceries — 3%

Milk, bread, produce, a bag of chips — anything you'd take home and prepare yourself gets Utah's special, reduced grocery rate: about 3%, far below the regular sales tax rate.

🍔

Prepared or restaurant food — full rate

A restaurant meal, a hot deli sandwich, or anything sold ready-to-eat gets taxed at the regular full sales tax rate instead — typically around 6% to 9%, depending on the city, roughly double to triple the grocery rate.

Worth knowing

Most states don't tax groceries at all. Utah is one of only about ten states left that still does — just at a reduced rate instead of the full one. There's been ongoing discussion at the Legislature about eliminating it entirely, but as of this page, no permanent change has passed.

Income Tax: Utah's Biggest Tax by Far

This is the one most people already know from their paycheck — but it's worth spelling out because it's actually Utah's single largest source of tax revenue, bigger than sales tax or property tax on their own.

💵

One flat rate for everyone

Utah charges a flat 4.45% on income — the same percentage whether you make $30,000 or $300,000 a year. Some states charge higher rates for higher earners (a "progressive" system); Utah doesn't.

🏢

Businesses pay the same rate too

Utah's corporate income tax is also a flat 4.45% — the state runs individual and business income tax at matching rates.

Worth knowing

Utah's constitution requires that every dollar of income tax revenue go specifically to public education and to services for children and people with disabilities — it legally can't be spent on roads, prisons, or general government operations. This is different from sales tax and property tax, which fund a much broader range of things.

Gas Tax: Paid at the Pump, Funds the Roads

Every time you fill up in Utah, part of what you pay isn't for the gas itself — it's a per-gallon tax built into the price, specifically earmarked for roads and transportation.

Unlike sales tax, this isn't a percentage of the price — it's a flat number of cents per gallon, so it doesn't go up or down when gas prices change. Utah's Legislature adjusts the exact rate periodically; it was cut by about 6 cents per gallon in the 2026 session.

Three ways Utah actually pays for roads

Gas tax isn't the whole story anymore — electric and hybrid vehicles barely buy any gas, so Utah has added two more funding streams alongside it.

⛽

Gas tax

The traditional per-gallon tax described above. Works fine for gas-powered cars — doesn't apply to EVs, since they never buy gas.

🔌

Road Usage Charge (RUC)

A per-mile fee, specifically for electric and hybrid vehicles, so they still pay their share toward road maintenance even though they skip the gas pump. In 2026 it's 1.25 cents per mile, capped at $180 for the year. A 2026 law (S.B. 242) also created a separate, higher-cap version specifically for heavy commercial electric vehicles.

🛍️

Local option transportation sales tax

Separate from the gas tax entirely — this is a small slice of the regular sales tax you already pay on everyday purchases, specifically set aside by local vote to fund roads and transit in that area instead of general city or county spending.

A Few More Worth Knowing

These don't touch most people every day the way income, sales, and property tax do — but they show up regularly in our coverage.

Alcohol — a markup, not a tax
Tap to reveal
Utah is a "control state" — the state itself owns and runs every liquor store (52 of them). Instead of a separate tax on wine and liquor, the state just marks up the price before selling it to you. That markup generated about $551 million in sales in FY2025, with roughly $204.5 million going to state and local programs like roads and public safety. Beer works differently — it's sold through regular private retailers and taxed the normal per-gallon way.
Cigarette Tax
Tap to reveal
A flat $1.70 tax on every pack of 20 cigarettes, built into the price at checkout — separate from the regular sales tax.
Severance Tax
Tap to reveal
A tax paid by companies that extract oil, gas, or minerals from the ground in Utah — a percentage of the value of what they pull out. This connects directly to a lot of our donor-network reporting, since extraction companies both pay this tax and frequently donate to the same legislators who set its rate.
No Estate or Inheritance Tax
Tap to reveal
Unlike some states, Utah doesn't tax what you inherit or what your estate passes on when you die. Worth knowing since people sometimes assume otherwise.

Back to Property Tax: How It Can Get Redirected

Now that you know property tax is really several governments' slices added together, here's the part our investigations focus on most: sometimes, a slice of that future growth gets set aside for a specific development project instead of going to the usual places — before residents ever get the kind of public hearing a tax increase requires.

The part almost nobody explains: why the county line can rise even when the county isn't "raising taxes"

Here's the mechanism that usually stays invisible. When a project area or PID freezes a chunk of growing property value out of the county's regular tax base, that frozen value gets subtracted from the math the county uses to calculate its rate for everyone else. Since the county still needs to collect the same total budgeted amount, and it's now doing that math against a smaller pool of taxable value, the rate itself has to rise slightly to make up the difference — and that higher rate applies to every other property in the county, not just the ones in the project area.

The key part: this doesn't legally count as the county "raising taxes." No Truth-in-Taxation hearing is triggered, because the county isn't collecting more total revenue than it budgeted for — the increase shows up quietly, baked into the rate calculation itself. So a resident can watch their county line item creep up year after year with no hearing, no notice, and no explanation of why, because the actual cause — a frozen tax base somewhere else in the county — was never disclosed to them in the first place.

Six more terms, tap a card to reveal each one.

Truth-in-Taxation
Tap to reveal
Utah's law requiring a public notice and hearing before a taxing entity can collect more than its certified rate would generate. This is the part of the system residents actually get to see and weigh in on.
Certified Rate
Tap to reveal
The tax rate that adjusts automatically so rising property values alone don't hand a government a windfall. If a government wants more than this generates, that's when Truth-in-Taxation kicks in.
Tax Increment (TIF)
Tap to reveal
The new tax revenue a project area generates above its starting baseline value. This is the part that can be redirected to fund development instead of going to schools, fire, water, and other ordinary services.
Project Area
Tap to reveal
A defined boundary — created by a state authority or a county — where growth's tax increment is committed to development for a set number of years, instead of flowing to the entities that would normally receive it.
PID
Tap to reveal
Public Infrastructure District — its own small government, created to serve a specific development, that can levy its own taxes and issue its own bonds to pay for that development's infrastructure.
CRA
Tap to reveal
Community Reinvestment Agency — a county or city tool similar to a project area, where growth's tax increment inside a designated boundary is redirected to fund redevelopment there instead of general services.

How the two systems actually differ

01

A tax increase asks the public first

When a school district, city, or water district wants to collect more than its certified rate, the law requires a public notice and a hearing. Residents get a date, a location, and the chance to show up and object before it happens.

02

Redirecting growth revenue usually doesn't

Creating a project area, a PID, or a CRA — and committing years of future growth revenue to it — is typically approved through ordinary board votes and interlocal agreements. There's no equivalent public notice requirement built in.

03

Both affect the same pool of money

Growth is supposed to help pay for the roads, water, sewer, and emergency response it requires. When a large share of that new revenue is set aside for a project area instead, the entities that provide those services are left covering more of the gap — sometimes by asking for a tax increase of their own.

A simple way to picture it

Think of it like your neighborhood's water bill. New neighbors move in and start paying for water too — that new money is supposed to help cover the shared system everyone uses. But under some of these deals, most of that new revenue is set aside for years to help pay for the new development itself, not the shared system. Meanwhile, your own water bill goes up because "the system needs more money."

Why this matters

None of this means every tax increase is caused by a project area, or that every development deal is a problem. Plenty of infrastructure genuinely needs upgrading, and plenty of development wouldn't happen without some form of public financing. What matters is that these two things — public tax increases and private redirection of growth revenue — usually happen through completely separate processes, on separate timelines, in front of separate audiences. Our reporting exists to put them back on the same page, so you can judge the pattern for yourself.

These Tools Aren't Mutually Exclusive — They Stack

Everything explained so far — PIDs, CRAs, RDAs, HTRZs, UIPA differentials — can exist as separate, standalone tools. But nothing stops a single development from having more than one layered on top of it at the same time. Each layer is created separately, through its own approval process, and each one keeps running on its own timeline even after the others are already in place.

What this adds up to

The PID line hits one homeowner's bill directly, every year. The CRA and HTRZ figures redirect revenue at the project or zone level — money that would otherwise flow to the county, city, school district, or general fund, now unavailable for those purposes for the life of the agreement. And on top of all of it, if the parcel also sits inside a state authority's project area, up to 75% of whatever new growth in property value it generates never reaches the ordinary taxing entities at all. None of these four numbers are drawn from one single real project — they're illustrative, built at a realistic scale from the mechanisms explained above. What's real is that all four can apply to the same ground at once, and each one is approved separately, without ever being shown together in one place.

A real, documented example — not hypothetical

Nordic Village, in Weber County, already has two of these stacked at once. A CRA commits the county's share of increment for 15 years, and the school district, fire district, and Weber Basin each commit their own share for 15 years too. Separately, a Nordic Village PID issued $39.38 million in its own bonds, carrying its own 6.5% rate through 2055. These are legally distinct tools, approved through separate processes — but they're financially stacked on the exact same development, and a resident living there is paying into both at once.

Each individual layer usually gets its own approval, at its own meeting, described in its own terms. What rarely gets shown in one place is how many of these a single project can carry simultaneously — which is exactly why the pattern is easy to miss unless someone adds it all up.

Worth sitting with

The more PIDs get added, the more of this shifts onto residents

Every PID, project area, and CRA that freezes growth revenue out of the county's base adds to the same rate-shifting effect explained above. Each one, on its own, can look small and defensible. Added together across years and across a growing county, the cumulative effect lands on ordinary residential tax bills — while the properties actually sitting inside these districts are the ones benefiting from the financing that created them.

These districts are consistently justified publicly as economic development, affordable housing, or a response to a housing crisis. Whether that framing holds up deserves real scrutiny against what a given project actually delivers, who actually occupies it, and what it actually costs residents outside its boundary — not taken at face value just because it was the stated reason at approval.

Bills That Changed These Rules

None of this is static — the Utah Legislature amends these mechanisms almost every session. Here are the specific bills behind the rules explained on this page, most recent first.

2026 General Session
S.B. 197 — Transportation Funding and Governance Amendments (Harper/Christofferson)
Rewrote the statute governing how growth revenue is divided among HTRZ, RDA, UFAIR, and Point of the Mountain Authority districts statewide.
S.B. 242 — Transportation Amendments (Harper/Christofferson)
Created the higher-cap Road Usage Charge for heavy commercial EVs; changed allowed uses of local option transportation sales tax.
H.B. 77 — Tax Modifications (Eliason/McCay)
Widened the automatic reassessment trigger for commercial property from 150% to 350%, while residential stayed at 150%.
S.B. 60 — Income Tax Rate Amendments (McCay/Eliason)
Cut the state income tax rate from 4.50% to 4.45%, the sixth consecutive year of cuts.
S.B. 206 — Tax Amendments (Harper/Walter)
Creates a new statewide TIF disclosure and database system covering UIPA, MIDA, UFAIR, and the Point of the Mountain Authority — but the disclosure timing was weakened from "before" to "within 30 days after" between substitutes.
H.B. 575 — Fuel Tax and Supply Amendments (Roberts)
Cut the state gas tax by about 15%, roughly 6 cents per gallon — also creates new pipeline permitting rules and a refinery data-reporting requirement with strong secrecy protections attached.
H.B. 106 — Income Tax Revisions (2025)
Cut the income tax rate from 4.55% to 4.50% (introduced at a bigger cut, to 4.45%, before being scaled back); also expanded the child tax credit and created a new employer child-care facility tax credit.
Earlier sessions
S.B. 333 — 2025 — Major Sporting Event Venue Zone framework
Created a new zone type that can redirect up to 75% of property tax increment — the same underlying model expanding into a new policy area.
S.B. 69 — 2024 — Income Tax Amendments
Cut the income tax rate from 4.65% to 4.55%.
H.B. 562 — 2024
Created UFAIR, the Utah Fairpark Area Investment and Restoration District.
S.B. 228 — 2019 — Public Infrastructure District Act
Established the modern legal framework for PIDs in Utah (Title 17D, Chapter 4) — the tool behind most of the PID examples on this page.

This list covers the bills directly behind the mechanisms explained on this page — not a complete record of every Utah tax bill. We'll update it as new sessions pass relevant legislation.

Graded Against the Collective Rubric

Every bill and structure covered by this project gets judged the same way — five fixed questions, applied consistently: does it add or remove a public check on power; can the public actually see the process; who pays and is that disclosed plainly; what's the environmental effect; and who bears the cost or gets real standing to be heard. Some of these bills have deep, documented process detail behind them (2026 session ones especially); a few older ones are graded on thinner information, flagged below.

A caution worth stating plainly: bills marked "Needs Review" below haven't had a full introduced-to-final redline pulled yet. Every bill on this page that HAS gotten that treatment turned out to have real substance a surface read missed. A clean-looking committee record isn't the same as a confirmed grade.

S.B. 197 (2026) D
Power/Transparency — Bad. The base-year amendment that reshaped how every HTRZ, RDA, and UFAIR district in the state divides growth revenue passed by anonymous voice vote — no named mover, no record. Financial accountability — Bad. Changes a statewide revenue formula with no public-facing explanation of who wins or loses from the two-year delay.
S.B. 242 (2026) D
Transparency — Public committee hearing, named opposition testimony on the record — unlike S.B. 197. Power — Passed over organized bike/pedestrian-safety and neighborhood opposition; a documented provision lets transit vehicles block a bike or parking lane while loading, unloading, or making an operational adjustment. Financial accountability — A consumer privacy protection present in the introduced version — barring towing dispatch vendors from selling or sharing a vehicle owner's personal information — was removed in the substitute that passed; the public record doesn't state why. This bill is also the specific statutory basis Weber County used for its new 0.2% "5th 5th" sales tax, a real, current, checkable fiscal effect.
H.B. 77 (2026) D
Financial accountability — Bad. Widened the automatic reassessment trigger for commercial property to more than double the residential threshold (350% vs. 150%), added via floor amendment rather than in the bill as introduced. Community impact — Bad. Shifts which properties get caught by faster tax review, favoring commercial over residential, with limited public conversation about that specific tradeoff.
S.B. 60 (2026) Needs Review
What's confirmed: real, named opposition testimony in both chambers' committees (four residents in the Senate; Moe Hickey of Voices for Utah Children in the House), with non-unanimous votes both times (4-1-2 Senate, 8-2-1 House). But no full introduced-to-final redline has been pulled for this bill yet — and every bill on this page that HAS gotten that treatment (H.B. 106, H.B. 575, S.B. 242, S.B. 206) turned out to have real substance a surface read missed. A clean-looking committee record isn't the same as a confirmed grade.
S.B. 206 (2026) C
Transparency — Mixed. Creates a new advance-disclosure requirement and public statewide database for TIF entities that didn't exist before — a real, substantive first for the exact mechanism (PID/TIF/UIPA/MIDA/UFAIR) this page spends most of its length examining. But between substitutes, the disclosure deadline was changed from before a TIF entity acts to within 30 days after — the public sees the plan after the fact, not before it. Financial accountability — Mixed. The defined self-declared spending cap ("maximum tax increment a local entity seeks to generate") was deleted and replaced with a looser, authorization-tied standard in the version that passed.
H.B. 575 (2026) D
Transparency — The tax-cut rate change itself is public and plainly disclosed. But the same bill creates a new refinery reporting requirement whose data is classified as a protected record, confidential commercial information, and a trade secret all at once — exempt from GRAMA, barred from use in any regulatory, civil, or criminal proceeding, not subject to subpoena or court order, with unlawful disclosure by a state employee a Class A misdemeanor. Power — Bundled with pipeline permitting streamlining (a 120-day hard deadline across seven DEQ chapters), a new state duty for UDOT to "cooperate with and assist" pipeline companies on siting, and an expansion of the High Cost Infrastructure tax credit to cover hydrocarbon storage in salt caverns — all in the same bill as the tax cut. Financial accountability — Confirmed $11,903,900 one-time General Fund appropriation to local B and C Roads. Community impact — While the gas tax drops to $0.319/gallon, the alternative-fuel tax rate (CNG/LNG/hydrogen) rises from a 16.5¢ cap to 21.2¢, the same session — gas gets cheaper, EV-adjacent fuels get more expensive, in the same bill.
H.B. 106 (2025) Needs Review
What's actually confirmed from the bill's own redline: the rate cut was scaled back during the process — introduced at 4.55% to 4.45%, passed at 4.55% to 4.50%, a smaller cut than Christofferson's own bill originally proposed. The bill also bundles three distinct provisions with three different beneficiary groups: the individual/corporate rate cut (broad), a new tax credit specifically for employers who build or operate child care facilities (business-only), and an expanded child tax credit for kids under six (parents of young children only). That's enough to state plainly, not enough to grade confidently on Power, Environmental impact, or Community impact — none of those have been examined yet.
S.B. 333 (2025) C-
Power — Mixed. Extends the same increment-capture model (up to 75% redirected) into a new category of zone, expanding a mechanism this page already treats critically into more territory. Financial accountability — Thin documentation on how the public was shown this expansion's tradeoffs at the time.
S.B. 69 (2024) Needs Review
Graded on process visibility alone so far — no redline comparison or committee minutes have actually been pulled for this bill. Same caution applies as S.B. 60: don't trust this grade until it gets the same full-text treatment the other bills on this page have gotten.
H.B. 562 (2024) Needs Review
Created UFAIR. We don't yet have the committee record, vote breakdown, or public testimony for this one — grading it now would be a guess dressed up as an assessment. Flagged for deeper research before a grade is assigned.
S.B. 228 (2019) D
Power/Financial accountability — Bad, structurally. This is the enabling statute for the entire PID mechanism examined throughout this page — every PID stacking example above traces back to this one bill. It created a tool whose core design (its own government, its own bonds, minimal ongoing public visibility once created) is the same structure this page spends most of its length raising concerns about. Graded on the tool it created, not on any specific project's execution of it.
