The $1.4 Million Assumption

Why Ogden Valley's city budget departed from its incorporation forecast: what the state's study assumed, what it warned about, what the city's own records show, and the Jan. 1 rule that kept the city from setting its own tax rate in its first year.

The short version

The study passed the city. LRB Public Finance Advisors, hired by the Lieutenant Governor's Office, found in May 2024 that the new city would "likely result in at least a five percent budget surplus."
It guessed sales tax would grow 9% a year. The study based that on statewide taxable sales since 2018. The city's advisor, Zions Public Finance, called it "based in part on COVID-era data." The city's budget uses 3%.
That is a $1,386,503 gap between two forecasts. It is not money the city counted and found missing. Startup costs, service levels and capital work are separate parts of the city's overall budget.
The study named its own risks. A thin commercial tax base, sales that couldn't be traced to an address, capital work it did not include, and trimmed engineering and planning costs.
A Jan. 1 rule blocked a first-year tax rate. The certificate came Jan. 2, 2026. A Tax Commission rule says a new city can't set a rate for a year unless it existed on Jan. 1. Spring Lake, in Utah County, was certified as a town Jan. 16 and hit the same rule.
The 512.6% plan was dropped. On Nov. 13, KSL reported a smaller budget of about $5.9 million instead, with a planned road fee and a 6% energy tax in the budget.

Where it is

Ogden Valley City covers Eden, Liberty and the unincorporated valley around Huntsville, which is its own town. Snowbasin and Powder Mountain's main resort areas are outside the city line. Part of Powder Mountain is in Cache County.

What the study warned about

Commercial landA lack of commercial or industrial land "can create pressure on the general fund over time." The study adds this is not unique to Ogden Valley.
Where sales happenOnly 33% of the area's sales tax could be traced to a physical address. The rest came from online sellers. LRB used "high-level assumptions," and the study says sales tax revenue "could be misrepresented as a result."
Capital improvementsThe study left out future capital improvements outside the county's existing service level. It says bridges and storm drains need repair and replacement either way.
Engineering and planningAfter talks with the county, LRB counted only part of those costs. The study says this "presents a certain risk."
New taxes the city could chooseThe study listed a tax on gas and electricity as an option. It did not estimate the amount.
A second look before the voteIn July 2024, the Utah Foundation, hired by Keep Our Valley Free, found the city would miss the 5% test with lower growth or a smaller sales tax base. It also said its work was "not a critique of the existing feasibility study," which it said made "logical and sensible choices."

The resorts outside the line

The people who asked for the city drew the boundary before the state's study began. The boundary left out the main resort areas of Snowbasin and Powder Mountain, although some parcels owned by resort-related companies are inside the city. LRB studied the boundary it was given and took out the resorts' estimated share of sales: 60% of ZIP 84317 for Snowbasin (plus 10% for Huntsville) and 20% of ZIP 84310 for Powder Mountain.

That is about $837,000 for 2026–2028. Don't subtract it from the $1,386,503: the years are different, and this math keeps the 9% guess. Including the resorts could have increased revenue, but this comparison does not show whether it would have ended the shortage.

The Jan. 1 cutoff and the court case

This part is separate from the study. The city sent its incorporation papers Dec. 17, 2025. A missing signature was fixed Dec. 23. The certificate was issued Jan. 2, 2026. The Tax Commission applied a Jan. 1 cutoff and denied the city that year's process for setting its own rate. The city challenged that reading.

The rule. Tax Commission rule R884-24P-24(12): no new entity, "including a new city," may have a certified tax rate or levy a tax for a year "unless that entity existed on the first day of that calendar year."
The ruling. On July 13, Third District Judge Todd Shaughnessy upheld the rule. KUER reported he wrote the late certificate was due to circumstances "essentially beyond the City's control."
Residents still pay county property tax. The county passes the city its share. The 2026 notices show that share at a rate of .000156.
The appeal. KSL reported Nov. 13 it was still pending. A Nov. 14 city statement said the Supreme Court asked both sides Sept. 25 whether the appeal is moot and has not decided whether to rule or dismiss. We could not check the court's online docket.
The 512.6% vote. Aug. 18, 3-2, on the city's part of the tax only. For the average $1.22 million home: $104.85 to $640.51 a year. Dropped Nov. 13.
What the city said. KSL reported in July that the draft budget called the study's projections "artificially inflated" and said the study "underestimated the startup expenses" of a new city.

The law and the bills

Utah Code 10-2a-205 (feasibility studies)Five-year projections; average revenue must beat average cost by more than 5%. Costs assume services that "fairly and reasonably approximate" what the area already gets. Sponsors pay the study cost for studies on or after May 1, 2024. We found no step for reviewing a study's forecast after a city starts in this section.
Tax Commission rule R884-24P-24(12)The Jan. 1 cutoff. An agency rule, not a law passed by the Legislature. Spring Lake in Utah County (certified as a town Jan. 16, 2026) hit it too. Lawmakers could change it by law.
2026 H.B. 510, Municipal Incorporation ModificationsPrimary sponsor Rep. Tiara Auxier; Senate sponsor Sen. Kirk Cullimore. Would have allowed county audits of feasibility studies for preliminary municipalities. Failed in the Senate March 6, 2026, 14-12-3.
2026 H.B. 592, Municipal Incorporation AmendmentsPrimary sponsor Rep. Mike Kohler. Preliminary municipalities: no new applications after Feb. 1, 2026; landowners pay for damage to county property. Returned to House Rules without being considered March 2; filed March 6.
2027 sessionCouncil member Kay Hoogland said the incorporation process could be a focus in 2027. Mayor Janet Wampler said in May she wants a bill making it easier for new cities to set a property tax in their first year.

Graded by the Collective Rubric

Transparency: StrongThe study, hearing notices, county notice, Zions slides, council notes and budgets are public, and the study named its risks in writing. Gaps: Cache-side fire payment terms and the signed loan papers are not public.
Financial accountability: WeakThe 9% growth rate leaned on years that included COVID, and the city's own advisor said so. The study left out future capital improvements and trimmed some costs, in writing. We found no requirement to review a forecast after a city starts. A 6% energy tax is in the budget, a road fee is planned, and road work was cut.
Process followed: MixedThe state hired the firm, held the hearings, and the voters decided. The certificate came one day after the Jan. 1 date the Tax Commission applied.

What the records do not show

  • The signed loan note, its final rate, due date and fees.
  • The Supreme Court's current docket entry.
  • Where individual resort sales, such as online tickets, are sourced.
  • What Cache County pays for fire service on its side of Powder Mountain.
  • The resorts' full property tax and the cost of serving them, so whether including them would have closed the gap.
  • How much startup costs, service levels and capital work each add to the city's overall funding need.

This page does not say anyone acted in bad faith, that the study broke the law, or that the 9% guess alone caused the city's money problems.
