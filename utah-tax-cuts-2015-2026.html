The count

63 laws lowered a tax or created a credit, exemption or relief from 2015 through 2026. 9 of them also raised a tax or fee in the same bill.

Looking for one law? All 63 cut laws has the searchable list, with what each did, who gets the relief, who pays, the sponsors, the votes and every source.

Who the income tax cuts reach

Utah had 1,439,252 full-year resident income tax returns in 2023. A flat rate cut lowers everyone's tax by the same share of taxable income, so filers with more taxable income save more dollars. Under H.B. 54 (2023), the rate change alone was about $100 on $50,000 of taxable income and about $400 on $200,000.

2023 income groupShare of returnsShare of individual income tax owedTax per return, compared with the average return
$50,000 or less45.8%5.1%About one-ninth of average (0.11 times)
Over $50,000 to $250,00049.4%58.0%A little above average (1.2 times)
Over $250,0004.8%36.9%About 7.7 times average
Why the middle group pays the biggest share. It is the largest group, almost half of all returns, so its combined total is the biggest: 58%. Per return, the middle group pays a little more than the average return, and filers over $250,000 pay about 7.7 times the average. Everyone pays the same rate on taxable income (4.65% for 2023). Filers with lower incomes owe less mainly because the taxpayer tax credit, which shrinks as income rises, cancels much of their tax. The last column is our arithmetic: each group's share of the tax divided by its share of returns.

Source: Utah State Tax Commission FY2025 Annual Report, individual income tax by income group, tax year 2023. This shows who owes the tax, not an exact split of any one cut; credits and phaseouts change the result for each household.

Which industries the business laws help

Each business law below shows who qualifies and what it requires. Where a law's text lists no jobs, wage or investment requirement, the list says so.

Energy, mining and heavy industry (10)
  • S.B. 216 (2015), High Cost Infrastructure Tax Credits. Who qualifies: Businesses with new or expanded industrial, mining, manufacturing, distribution or business-services projects (retail excluded), or $50M+ new investment in an existing qualifying entity. Required: Infrastructure cost must exceed 10% of total project cost or $10M; agreement with Office of Energy Development; credit = 30% of infrastructure-related revenue, capped at 50% of infrastructure construction cost, over up to 20 years; 7-year carryforward; certificate required.
  • S.B. 102 (2016), High Cost Infrastructure Tax Credit Amendments. Who qualifies: Infrastructure cost-burdened entities approved under the HCITC program. Required: Per Office of Energy Development rules.
  • S.B. 17 (2016), Revenue and Taxation Amendments. Who qualifies: Oil and gas producers. Required: none stated.
  • S.B. 197 (2017), Refinery Sales and Use Tax Exemption Amendments. Who qualifies: Utah petroleum refiners. Required: From July 1, 2021, refiner must obtain Office of Energy Development certification and report average gasoline sulfur of 10 ppm or less (per Ryan summary).
  • H.B. 124 (2024), Energy Infrastructure Amendments. Who qualifies: Energy, mining/mineral processing, utilities and industrial facilities with qualifying infrastructure. Required: HCITC program criteria.
  • H.B. 373 (2024), Environmental Quality Amendments. Who qualifies: Businesses buying pollution control equipment (incl. oil/gas and refiners). Required: Certification required.

How the cuts are paid for: what the records show

1. The biggest cuts came in surplus years. Utah ended fiscal 2022 with a $1.3 billion surplus, and lawmakers projected more than $3.3 billion going into 2023. That is when the two largest rate cuts passed (S.B. 59 and H.B. 54). Rep. Steve Eliason said the surplus could pay for the relief without cutting other spending; Voices for Utah Children said the money would do more in schools and other programs.
KSL, Nov. 21, 2022
2. A rate cut keeps costing every year after the surplus is gone. A surplus is extra money in one year. A rate cut lowers what the state collects every year from then on. So the first year can be covered by extra money, but the years after must be covered by growth in the economy, by spending less than planned, or by other revenue. Reporting in December 2025 put five years of state income tax cuts at about $1.4 billion less revenue each year. That month the governor called the next budget "a flat year" and did not recommend another rate cut, partly because federal tax changes were expected to cost Utah about $500 million in income tax over 2026 and 2027. Most income tax, by the Utah Constitution, pays for public schools.
Utah News Dispatch, Dec. 4, 2025 · KUTV, Dec. 4, 2025 (education leaders' concerns; that story ties the drop to the federal changes)
3. Some bills made up part of the cost inside the same bill. In these bills, one tax went down and another went up at the same time, so the people who got the cut were not always the same people who paid the new tax.
  • H.B. 293 (2018): income taxpayers paid about $52.9 million less the first year (money the Education Fund did not collect). In the same bill, property owners paid about $36.1 million more, because the state school property tax rate was held in place instead of dropping, plus $18.6 million from a new school rate. That property tax money also goes to schools.
  • H.B. 301 (2023): drivers buying gas paid a lower gas tax. In the same bill, most vehicle owners pay $7 more a year to register, and people charging electric cars at public chargers pay a new 12.5% tax. The fiscal note still shows less money overall for the Transportation Fund.
  • S.B. 2001 (2019): lowered the income tax and raised the tax on food, gas and some services. It was repealed after a citizen referendum petition, before it took effect.
Each bill's fiscal note, linked on its row below
4. Some state laws move costs to local taxpayers. Income tax is a state tax, so the income tax cuts do not come out of city and county budgets. Property tax is different. Every local taxing body (the county, the city, the school district and special districts like water, fire or sewer) sets its rate to collect a budgeted amount. When the state exempts some property, that property drops out of the tax base, and the same budget gets spread over everyone else's property, unless the local body lowers its budget.
  • H.B. 231 (2019) raised the business equipment exemption from $10,000 to $15,000 per county. The fiscal note says about $88.5 million in taxable value moved onto all other taxable property in those taxing areas, homes and businesses alike: about $0.46 more a year on a $250,000 home and $3.36 more on $1 million of business property. Businesses that qualified saved at most about $145 per owner per county.
  • S.B. 18 (2021) raised that exemption again, to $25,000 per county. The fiscal note says businesses would pay about $2 million less a year (about $56 each on average) and local governments could collect about $2 million less, starting in fiscal 2023. That $2 million is the statewide total across every county, city, school district and special district that taxes business equipment. The note does not say whether it shifts to other owners or is simply not collected.
  • H.B. 151 (2023) raised the disabled veterans' exemption. The fiscal note says about $4.7 million a year shifts to other property: about $2.99 more on a $400,000 home and $13.60 more on $1 million of business property.
Zones and authorities that keep property tax growth, like MIDA under S.B. 269 (2019), take growth that local taxing bodies would otherwise receive.
Fiscal notes for each bill
5. What the records do not show. Local governments raised property taxes too: 68 entities, about $96 million, in 2026. The records show those increases happened, but they do not tie them to the state's cuts. Each local body gives its own reasons. This page puts cuts and increases side by side; it does not say one paid for the other unless a record says so.

Rules that shift costs or limit review

Beyond the dollar amounts, some of these laws contain rules that change who pays, for how long, or who can question it. Each item below is what the bill text or fiscal note says.

No jobs, wage or investment requirement in the text readThe data center sales tax exemption (S.B. 3002 in 2016, rewritten by S.B. 114 in 2020) sets only size and new-construction tests. No such condition was found in S.B. 17 (2016, oil and gas), S.B. 156 (2024, radioactive waste), S.B. 243 (2024, aircraft property), S.B. 219 (2025, financial institutions) or H.B. 60 (2025, pass-through owners). By contrast, EDTIF credits are paid only after a company meets job and wage targets, and H.B. 35 (2022) set 2,500 jobs and $1 billion in investment for its largest credit.
Costs moved to other property ownersH.B. 151 (2023), H.B. 231 (2019) and S.B. 18 (2021), as described above. Each fiscal note says the relief for one group is paid by other property owners or local governments.
Tax growth kept for decadesS.B. 269 (2019) lets MIDA keep up to 75% of the property tax growth in its project areas for 25 years per parcel, plus up to 15 more if its board finds "significant benefit." The increases page lists 28 more laws that give taxing power or tax growth to zones, districts and authorities.
Publicly owned, privately run, not taxedS.B. 269 (2019) made MIDA-owned hotels and hotel and commercial condo units exempt from property tax and privilege tax, even when a private company runs them under a long-term agreement.
Time limits on challengesS.B. 269 (2019) bars any challenge to a MIDA project area unless it is filed within 30 days, and bars all challenges to project areas created before Dec. 1, 2018. For project areas created from Dec. 1, 2018 to May 14, 2019, challenges were barred after July 1, 2019.
Who decides the boundariesS.B. 269 (2019) says a city may not annex land inside a MIDA project area without MIDA's approval, and lets MIDA petition to annex land to a city as if it were the only private owner. It deletes an earlier rule that let the military installation object to those petitions within 14 days. It also makes MIDA the local authority for alcohol licenses in its project areas.
Different rules for homes and other propertyH.B. 77 (2026) has county assessors review a home's value when it jumps 150% in a year, but other property only when it jumps 350%.
Cost not measuredThe fiscal note for S.B. 114 (2020) lists the data center exemption's cost as "unknown." See the Data-Centers repo's S.B. 114 case file.
Records made privateH.B. 325 (2026) lets a government office make private any part of a record showing whether a taxpayer gets the blind persons' property tax exemption or relief under Title 59, Chapter 2a ("Tax Relief Through Property Tax"). The enrolled text does not name the 45% primary residential exemption or business abatements.
Little time to reviewS.B. 269 (2019) was substituted and passed by the Senate the same night, under suspended rules, and its fiscal note was received that same day. It passed both chambers seven days after it was numbered.
